# Weak-to-Strong Generalization: Eliciting Strong Capabilities With Weak Supervision (W2S)
[![Kaggle](https://kaggle.com/static/images/open-in-kaggle.svg)](https://www.kaggle.com/code/nadymsazad/weak-to-strong-generalization)

- **Paper:** [Weak-to-Strong Generalization: Eliciting Strong Capabilities With Weak Supervision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.09390)
- **Authors:** Collin Burns, Pavel Izmailov, Jan Hendrik Kirchner, Bowen Baker, Leo Gao, Leopold Aschenbrenner, Yining Chen, Adrien Ecoffet, Manas Joglekar, Jan Leike, Ilya Sutskever, Jeff Wu
- **Lab, year:** OpenAI, 2023 (preprint)

## Summary

In December 2023, researchers at OpenAI introduced **weak-to-strong generalization**, a framework for studying how to align superhuman models when humans cannot reliably evaluate their outputs. The paper asks a simple question: if we train a strong model using labels generated by a weak model, can the strong model still perform better than its weak supervisor?

The authors test this using pretrained language models from the GPT-4 family (the same architecture and pretraining data as GPT-4, spanning 7 orders of magnitude in compute) across three settings: 22 popular NLP classification benchmarks, chess puzzles, and ChatGPT reward modeling. They find that naively fine-tuning strong models on weak labels consistently yields performance above the weak supervisor — a phenomenon they call weak-to-strong generalization. On NLP tasks, fine-tuning GPT-4 with GPT-2-level supervision recovers about half the performance gap. However, the gap is not fully closed, suggesting that naive RLHF will scale poorly to superhuman models.

The paper also shows that simple methods can dramatically improve generalization. An auxiliary confidence loss (conditional entropy minimisation) increases PGR from about 25% to nearly 80% on NLP tasks when using the smallest supervisor with the largest student. Bootstrapping with intermediate model sizes helps on chess puzzles. These results suggest that empirical progress on superalignment is tractable today.

This notebook implements the weak-to-strong learning setup with simple MLP classifiers on a synthetic classification task, measures PGR, and tests the auxiliary confidence loss.

## What Problem Does It Solve

Imagine you are a manager who needs to evaluate the work of an employee who is much smarter than you. You can tell whether their work is good or bad some of the time, but for the hardest problems they solve, you honestly cannot tell if their solution is correct. How do you supervise someone whose abilities exceed your own?

This is the core challenge of aligning superhuman AI: when models become smarter than humans, human feedback (the basis of RLHF) becomes unreliable. The paper proposes an analogy — replace the weak human supervisor with a weak model supervisor, and the superhuman model with a strong model. If a weak model's labels can still elicit the capabilities of a much stronger model, then there is hope that human supervision can still guide superhuman models. The key insight is that the strong model already knows the right answer from pretraining; the weak supervisor just needs to elicit it, not teach it.

## Core Idea

- **Weak-to-strong learning setup:** Train a weak model on ground-truth labels, generate its predictions on a held-out set (weak labels), then fine-tune a strong model on those weak labels. Measure performance against ground truth to see if the strong model exceeds its weak supervisor.
- **Performance Gap Recovered (PGR):** A normalised metric: PGR = (weak-to-strong accuracy − weak accuracy) / (strong ceiling accuracy − weak accuracy). PGR = 1 means full recovery; PGR = 0 means no improvement over the weak supervisor.
- **Auxiliary confidence loss:** Add a conditional entropy minimisation term to the training loss, encouraging the strong model to be confident in its own predictions even when they disagree with weak labels. This reduces imitation of supervisor errors.

## Key Method Details

- **Models:** GPT-4 family models spanning 7 orders of magnitude in pretraining compute, from GPT-2-level to GPT-4 (Section 4.1)
- **Tasks:** 22 NLP binary classification benchmarks (ethics, commonsense, NLI, sentiment), chess puzzles (predict best first move), ChatGPT reward modeling (predict pairwise preferences)
- **Naive results (Section 4.2):** On NLP, naive PGR is ~20–50%, increasing with supervisor and student size. On chess, PGR is positive for small gaps but decreases with larger student models (inverse scaling). On reward modeling, PGR is only ~10%.
- **Confidence loss (Section 4.3.2):** Increases median NLP PGR from ~25% to ~80% for the largest gap. Related to conditional entropy minimisation (Grandvalet & Bengio, 2004).
- **Bootstrapping (Section 4.3.1):** Using intermediate model sizes to bridge large gaps improves PGR on chess puzzles.

### Simplifications in This Notebook

- **Smaller models:** Weak model = 1-hidden-layer MLP with 8 units (~186 parameters); Strong model = 3-hidden-layer MLP with 128 units (~37,250 parameters)
- **Dataset:** 10,000 synthetic 30-dimensional examples with 5 pairwise feature interactions, instead of 22 NLP benchmarks / chess / reward modeling
- **Pretrain 20 epochs** on a 3,000-example subset + **fine-tune 2 epochs** with Adam (lr 1e-3, weight decay 1e-4), instead of GPT-4-scale fine-tuning
- **Confidence loss weight:** 0.05, applied as conditional entropy minimisation

### What the Notebook Result Shows

On the Kaggle GPU run, the weak model (1-layer MLP, 8 hidden units) achieves about 78% test accuracy on the synthetic task. The pretrained strong model starts at about 85%, and the strong ceiling (fine-tuned on ground truth) reaches about 87%. The naive strong student fine-tuned on weak labels achieves about 79% test accuracy with a PGR of approximately 0.16, confirming weak-to-strong generalization — the strong student outperforms its weak supervisor despite being trained only on noisy labels.

With the auxiliary confidence loss, PGR improves to approximately 0.17–0.19 (the improvement is modest, mirroring the paper's finding that the confidence loss helps more for larger weak-strong gaps). The imitation analysis shows the confidence-loss model agrees slightly less with the supervisor on inputs where the supervisor is wrong. These are toy results with synthetic data and small MLPs, not comparable to the paper's GPT-4-scale NLP results (~50% naive PGR, ~80% with confidence loss). The qualitative findings — weak-to-strong generalization occurs, the confidence loss helps, and full recovery remains elusive — mirror the paper's conclusions.

## Influence

The paper was released alongside OpenAI's Superalignment team announcement and became a foundational reference for empirical superalignment research. It influenced follow-up work on scalable oversight methods, including studies of sandwiching, debate, and AI safety via debate. The weak-to-strong framework was adopted by other labs as a practical methodology for studying alignment scaling.
