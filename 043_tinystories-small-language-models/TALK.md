## Q&A (answers from the paper)

These answers come from the paper itself, not from an interview. Direct quotes are marked with their section in the [arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759).

**Q1: Why did the authors create a synthetic dataset instead of using existing corpora?**
A1 (from the paper, Section 1): "When we train a model on Wikipedia, for example, we are not only teaching it how to speak English, but also how to encode and retrieve an immense amount of facts and concepts from various domains and disciplines. Could it be that SLMs are overwhelmed by the amount and variety of information they have to process and store, and that this hinders their ability to learn the core mechanisms and principles of language?"

**Q2: How did the authors ensure the dataset was diverse enough?**
A2 (from the paper, Section 2): "In order to address the problem of creating a diverse dataset, we collected a vocabulary consisting of about 1500 basic words, which try to mimic the vocabulary of a typical 3-4 year-old child, separated into nouns, verbs, and adjectives. In each generation, 3 words are chosen randomly (one verb, one noun, and one adjective). The model is instructed to generate a story that somehow combines these random words into the story."

**Q3: How small can the models be and still produce coherent text?**
A3 (from the paper, Abstract): "TinyStories can be used to train and evaluate LMs that are much smaller than the state-of-the-art models (below 10 million total parameters), or have much simpler architectures (with only one transformer block), yet still produce fluent and consistent stories with several paragraphs that are diverse and have almost perfect grammar, and demonstrate reasoning capabilities."

**Q4: What evaluation paradigm did the authors introduce?**
A4 (from the paper, Section 3): "We suggest a framework which uses GPT-4 to grade the content generated by these models as if those were stories written by students and graded by a (human) teacher. This new paradigm overcomes the flaws of standard benchmarks which often require the model's output to be very structured, and moreover provides a multidimensional score for the model, providing scores for different capabilities such as grammar, creativity and consistency."

**Q5: Did the models just memorize the training data?**
A5 (from the paper, Section 4.4): "Typical k-grams in generated completions rarely appear in the dataset, for values of k as small as 4 or 5." The authors performed Rouge score analysis showing generated stories have very low overlap with the training set, and tested out-of-distribution instruction-following with instruction combinations absent from the training data, finding the models "produce genuinely novel and diverse stories, rather than simple variations of existing stories."

**Q6: What did the authors find about width vs depth?**
A6 (from the paper, Section 8): "Width is more important for capturing factual knowledge whereas depth is more important for contextual tracking." They also found that "grammar and syntactic abilities appear earlier than the ability to produce consistent text, which in turn appears ahead of ability to generate content that would be considered as creative."

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"TinyStories proves small models are as good as large models"** — The paper shows small models can produce coherent English when trained on appropriately scoped data, not that they match large models on complex tasks. The authors note their largest model (~80M) "falls short of GPT-4's abilities in terms of creativity quite significantly" (Section 3.1).

2. **"The TinyStories dataset is just random GPT-3.5 output"** — The dataset uses careful prompt engineering for diversity: random word combinations from a 1500-word vocabulary, random features (dialogue, twist, moral), and stories restricted to vocabulary a 3-4-year-old understands. Without this, simple repeated prompting produces "a very repetitive dataset, whose diversity is very far from what is required" (Section 2).

3. **"GPT-Eval is just asking GPT-4 if the text is good"** — GPT-Eval provides a structured, multidimensional assessment with specific scores for grammar, creativity, and consistency (each out of 10), plus an estimated "age group" of the writer. It uses ~50 manually prepared prompts and averages scores over 10 completions per prompt (Section 3).
